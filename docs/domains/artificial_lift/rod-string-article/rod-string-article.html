Rod string mechanics: what stretch, load and damping do to the dynamometer card

On a public example well, most of the lost stroke is plain rod stretch, the top of the thinner rods is the most stressed point, and the rod data and load-cell calibration, not the damping, decide the pump card.

At a glance: the objective, what we did and what we found.

A rod pump is diagnosed from the surface card, but the pump works at the far end of thousands of feet of steel rod. The rods stretch under the fluid load, carry stress waves at the speed of sound in steel and lose energy to the fluid around them on every stroke. The card data contains all of this information, so the rods have to be taken out of the card before the pump can be read. To keep the problem simple, this article takes the rod string on its own.

This is the second article of our rod pump series. On the series' public example well, we follow the stroke, the load and the stress down the rods, test how much the damping estimate matters and show where the calculation breaks down. You get the method, the public data and a reusable prompt to rerun it. The pumping unit and other component anomalies are the subjects of later articles in this series, and practical diagnostics and repairs follow the engineering.

Based on public data, Figure 1 shows the measured surface card of the example well and the cards computed from it at the taper joint, 1,400 ft down, and at the end of the rods, 4,750 ft down.

Key conclusions

The key conclusions for the example well are as follows:

  • Rod stretch takes about 90 % of the stroke lost between the surface (54.0 in) and the pump (39.6 in); the 3/4 in rods give 76.5 % of it.
  • The top of the 3/4 in rods is the most stressed point: 21,570 psi peak, 6.0 % above the polished rod, using about 46 % of the Grade D allowable stress range.
  • Damping hardly changes this pump card: ten times the estimate moves it by 1.3 % of its load range.
  • A 1 % larger rod diameter lowers the maximum pump load by about 3.2 %, and a 1 % load-cell gain error raises it by about 2.6 % (Appendix A).

Where the stroke goes

When the traveling valve closes, the fluid load moves from the tubing to the rods, and the rods stretch before the plunger moves. A rod stretches by its load times its length over its area and modulus [5]. Under the 3,310 lbf fluid load of the computed pump card, the two tapers stretch 3.0 in and 9.9 in, 12.9 in in all, against 14.4 in of stroke lost. Figure 2 compares the stroke down the rods with static stretch alone.

The lines end 1.5 in apart at the pump, and still 1.45 in with damping set to zero: the gap is the rods' motion, which the static formula leaves out.

Load and stress down the rods

Each foot of rod carries the load below it, so the load falls with depth; the stress is that load over the rod's area. Figure 3 shows the stress over the stroke at every depth.

The peak load falls from 12,240 lbf at the polished rod to 9,531 lbf at the taper joint, but there it acts on the smaller 3/4 in rods, so the peak stress steps up 36.1 % to 21,570 psi. The well record gives Grade D rods, minimum tensile strength 115,000 psi. On the modified Goodman diagram [6] at a service factor of 1, the allowable stress there is about 34,630 psi, and the top of the 3/4 in rods uses about 46 % of its allowable stress range, the 7/8 in rods about 35 %.

Damping and the pump card

Damping is the energy the rods lose to the fluid, and it makes the pump card's area smaller than the surface card's. With damping set to zero, the two areas agree within 0.005 %, an energy check on the calculation. The library's estimate, from the drag of the rods and couplings in the tubing, takes 0.7 % of the work per stroke. Figure 4 shows the pump card at three damping levels.

Ten times the damping raises the bottom of the card from 82 lbf to 213 lbf; the maximum pump load changes by 0.34 % and the fillage from 60.1 % to 59.4 %.

How the pump card is recovered

The finite-difference method marches the damped wave equation down the rods, one grid point at a time, from the measured position and load at the polished rod [2]. It is stable only while a stress wave, at about 16,300 ft/s in steel [3], takes at least one time step of the card to cross one grid step [4]: on this well's 200-point grid, at most 6,380 samples a stroke, against the 187 of the example card. One sample over the limit, the minimum pump load reaches −2,411 lbf with no flag; the library check is in review, so a high-rate card should be resampled first (Appendix C).

Appendix A Example well, stretch, stress and sensitivity

The example well and the stretch and stress results are summarised in this appendix.

  • The well is one anonymised field well in the digitalmodel library: a 1.500 in plunger on 1,400 ft of 7/8 in and 3,350 ft of 3/4 in Grade D steel rods, pumped at 6.42 strokes a minute; its survey is vertical. The end of the listed rods, 4,750 ft, is taken as the pump, which the record puts at 4,844 ft, and the tubing is taken as anchored (both ASSUMED - to be confirmed).
  • The record's stresses at the top of the 3/4 in rods are about 1,620 psi above ours, peak and minimum alike: a constant 717 lbf of load, close to the buoyancy of the 3/4 in rods below the joint (650 to 710 lbf), which our calculation deducts.
  • A 1 % larger rod diameter lowers the maximum pump load by about 3.2 % and the peak stress at the top of the 3/4 in rods by about 2.5 %; a 1 % load-cell gain error raises them by about 2.6 % and 1.3 %. Factors at ±1 % and ±5 % are given for nine inputs.

Inputs, results, the Goodman check, sensitivity and assumptions in full: digitalmodel on GitHub, stroke-and-stress.md.

Appendix B Damping

The key conclusion of the damping study is given in this appendix.

  • With no damping the pump card's area equals the surface card's within 0.005 %; the estimated damping takes 0.7 % of the work per stroke.
  • For this well, across a ten-fold range of damping, the maximum pump load changes by 0.34 % and the stroke by 0.03 in; the damping changes the card's area and the bottom of the card.
  • The estimate scales with the assumed viscosity, which is not calibrated; a heavier oil or a measured pump card can change this conclusion.

Damping study in full: digitalmodel on GitHub, damping.md.

Appendix C Method and its limits

How the pump card is recovered, and where the method breaks down, is summarised in this appendix.

  • The finite-difference method marches the damped wave equation down the rods from the surface card [2]; the frequency-domain method of Gibbs [1] is the library's second method.
  • The march is stable up to 6,380 samples a stroke on this well's 200-point grid; above it the pump card grows without bound, and the library check is in review.
  • The well is treated as vertical; the tubing is taken as anchored (ASSUMED - to be confirmed); rod-on-tubing friction is not modelled.

Method, stability limit and open items in full: digitalmodel on GitHub, method-and-limits.md.
