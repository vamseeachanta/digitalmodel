Dynamometer card analysis: from the surface card to the pump card and a diagnosis

A surface card, measured at the top of a rod-pumped well, carries enough to see the pump at the bottom of the well and to screen that pump from the office.

A rod pump works at the bottom of the well, out of sight. What the operator measures is the surface card: the load on the polished rod against its position, over one stroke. That card mixes the pump's work with the weight, stretch and vibration of the rod string, so a pump problem can hide inside it.

The capability shown here takes the rod string out of the picture. From the surface card and the well's rod and pump data, it computes the pump card, reads how far the plunger travels, how full the pump fills and what load it lifts, and gives a first reading of the pump's condition. It runs in a web page, so a well can be screened from the office and the well visit planned around a specific check.

Based on public data, two of the cards are shown in Figure 1.

  • The blue loop is the surface card of a public example well, measured at the top of the rod string.
  • The orange loop is the pump card computed from it, at the bottom of the 4,750 ft rod string.
  • The rods stretch and vibrate, so the 54.0 in surface stroke becomes a 39.6 in stroke at the pump.

Key conclusions

The key conclusions for the example well are as follows:

  • On this shutdown card the pump fills to 60 % of its stroke: 40.0 bbl/day at 24 hours a day and no slip, against 66.6 bbl/day for a full pump. The 27 bbl/day difference describes this card only; fillage in normal operation is not established. The cause, whether inflow, gas, valve leakage or a corner-detection error, is a check for the well.
  • The plunger lifts a fluid load of 3,310 lbf on each upstroke.
  • The pump stroke is 39.6 in, 73 % of the surface stroke. Rod stretch and rod dynamics take the rest, which the surface card alone does not separate.
  • The card shape reads as tagging up, the plunger striking at the top of its stroke. This reading is a hypothesis to check at the well; the pump card, the fillage and the fluid load are the result.
  • The well record marks this card as a shutdown card that the source system could not analyse, so the fillage and the fluid load rest on this calculation alone.

Two numerical methods are available for the pump card: a finite-difference method and a frequency-domain method. On this well their pump cards agree closely, within 0.26 % of the load range. Both are in the reusable prompt; Appendix B compares them, and Appendix A gives the inputs and limits of the example well.

Ten pump conditions read from their surface cards

Based on public data, ten common pump conditions are run through the same analysis, from surface card to pump card to a reading of the pump's condition. Each surface card is built from a published card shape, taken as a pump card by assumption, at an assumed stroke and load, so the cards illustrate shapes rather than field wells. They are shown in Figure 2.

  • Every pump card is recovered within 0.4 to 2.4 % of its load range.
  • The shape model names 6 of the 10 conditions correctly.
  • Where it misses, it reads tagging up, tagging down, plunger out of barrel and sand production as a traveling valve leak.

How the ten examples are built, and the full values, are given in Appendix C.

Run the analysis on your own card

The same analysis runs in a web page in the digitalmodel library (version of 14 Nov 2026); it needs no installation and no network once its files are on disk. Load a surface card with the well's rod and pump data, and the page draws the pump card and reads fillage, fluid load and the model readings.

  • The well record labels this card a shutdown card. The source system reports that its own corner detection, which finds the points where the valves open and close, failed on it, so the fillage and the fluid load rest on the corner detection of the digitalmodel library alone.
  • The rod string in the record ends at 4,750 ft and the pump is at 4,844 ft. The end of the rod string is taken as the pump; the 94 ft between them is not represented.
  • The shape model gives tagging up a model score of 99.91 %. The score is the model's own output, not the chance that the fault is present. The model is trained on synthetic cards; on the ten plate shapes it is correct on 6 of 10 (5 of the 8 whose plate label has the same name as a model class). The plate shapes also shape the synthetic training cards, so they are not an independent test.
  • The inputs that matter most: a 1 % larger rod diameter lowers the maximum pump load by about 3.1 % and the fillage by about 0.9 %; a 1 % load-cell gain error raises the maximum pump load by about 2.6 %; a 1 % surface position scale error moves the pump stroke by about 1.3 %. The full sensitivity table is in the full study.
  • Limits: unanchored tubing is not modelled and only steel rods are accepted. Both methods treat the well as vertical. A card alone does not give production; run time and slip are needed. The web page assumes a vertical well, anchored tubing and samples evenly spaced in time. Fluid load and fillage step with the detected corner samples, so rounding the last digit of a card can move the fluid load by tens of lbf: 34 lbf at most in the ten examples.

Appendix B The two numerical methods compared

The two methods that compute the pump card are compared in this appendix.

  • Finite-difference method, the default: it marches the damped wave equation for axial motion through the tapered rod string, after Everitt and Jennings (1992) [2].
  • Frequency-domain method: it solves the rod model of Gibbs (1963) [1] harmonic by harmonic, with 15 harmonics, and treats the well as vertical.
  • The well record also stores a pump card from the source system's own finite-difference calculation.

Table B1. Root-mean-square load difference between pump cards.

The two methods agree to 0.26 % of the load range, and each is within 0.78 % of the stored card. The stored card uses damping close to that of the default method, so its agreement with that method is a regression check; the frequency-domain method is the independent one. All results in this article use the default method.

Appendix C The ten examples in detail

How the ten examples are built and their full values are given in this appendix.

  • Each of the ten shapes of the public diagnostic plate [3] is scaled to an assumed 40 in pump stroke and to the static fluid load of a 1.5 in plunger at 4,844 ft with 100 psi tubing pressure and zero pump intake pressure (3,936 lbf). These are shape illustrations, not field pump cards. The plate gives shapes only, with no stroke or load, and does not say whether they are surface or pump cards; they are taken as pump cards by assumption.
  • Each scaled shape is lifted to a surface card through the rod string of the example well and then analysed like a measured card. The surface cards are therefore synthesised, not measured.
  • The plate labels insufficient supply and sand production have no model class of the same name; they are matched to fluid pound and sand wear by assumption.
  • The shape model names 6 of the 10 conditions; the second, shipped model names 4. Model scores are the models' own outputs, not the chance that a fault is present.
  • The web page reproduces the library on all ten examples: pump loads within 3 × 10⁻⁹ lbf and the same model labels.
