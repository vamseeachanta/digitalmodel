Dynamometer card analysis: from the surface card to the pump card and a diagnosis

A surface card, measured at the top of a rod-pumped well, carries enough to see the pump at the bottom of the well and to screen that pump from the office.

Diagnosing a rod pump is always a challenge. The pump works at the bottom of the well, out of sight. Some wells measure the downhole card, but most measure only the surface card: the load on the polished rod against its position, over one stroke. The card data contains all of this information at once: the pump's work, the weight, stretch and vibration of the rod string, the pumping unit, even loose bolts, so a pump problem can hide inside it.

Our troubleshooting tool computes the pump card from the surface card and gives a first reading of the pump's condition. To keep the problem simple, it models the rod string only to remove its effect from the card. The rod string, the pumping unit and other component anomalies are the subjects of later articles in this rod pump series, and practical diagnostics and repairs follow the engineering.

  • Check a well from the office, and plan the visit around a specific check.
  • The more suitable data you give it, with fewer assumptions, the better the pump card and the diagnosis.
  • Reusable prompt to try a public example well or perform own data custom investigations.

Some background, for readers who want it. A new well often flows on its own: the reservoir pressure pushes oil, water and gas up the tubing to the surface. As the well produces, the reservoir pressure declines, until it can no longer lift the fluid to the surface. The well then needs artificial lift, a pump or injected gas that adds the energy the reservoir no longer supplies.

The main artificial lift methods are the rod pump, the electric submersible pump, gas lift, the progressing cavity pump, plunger lift and the hydraulic pump; each suits different rates, depths and well conditions. In a rod pump, a pumping unit at the surface strokes a string of steel rods, and the rods drive a plunger pump at the bottom of the well. The surface card records the load on that rod string over each stroke.

That is the background. The rest of the article works with the rod pump.

Based on public data, the surface card of an example well and the pump card computed from it are shown in Figure 1.

  • The blue loop is the surface card, measured at the top of the rod string.
  • The orange loop is the pump card computed from it, at the bottom of the 4,750 ft rod string.
  • The rods stretch and vibrate, so the 54.0 in surface stroke becomes a 39.6 in stroke at the pump.

Key conclusions

The key conclusions for the example well are as follows:

  • On this card the pump fills to 60 % of its stroke (fillage: effective plunger travel as a share of the pump stroke). At this card's fillage, 24 hours a day and no slip, it displaces 40.0 bbl/day, against 66.6 bbl/day for a full pump, so about 27 bbl/day of pump capacity is not used.
  • Fillage in normal operation is not established. The only card is a shutdown card, the card the pump controller stores when it stops the pump, and the source system's own corner detection, which finds where the valves open and close, failed on it. Its cause, whether inflow, gas, valve leakage or a corner-detection error, is a check for the well.
  • The plunger lifts a fluid load of 3,310 lbf on each upstroke.
  • The pump stroke is 39.6 in, 73 % of the surface stroke. Rod stretch and rod dynamics take the rest.
  • The shape model, a classifier that gives each of 18 pump conditions a class probability from the card's shape, reads this card as tagging up, the plunger striking at the top of its stroke. The same model missed tagging up in its own ten-example test (Example 5, Figure 2), so this is a weak hypothesis to check at the well. It is independent of the fillage, which comes from the valve points on the pump card, not from the card's shape.

Two numerical methods are available for the pump card: a finite-difference method [2] and a frequency-domain method [1]. On this well their pump cards differ by 0.26 % (root mean square) of the load range, the span from the smallest to the largest load on the card, so for this problem the two methods are equivalent. Appendices A and B give the example well and the comparison.

Pump condition diagnostics from surface cards

Based on public data, ten common pump conditions are run through the same analysis. Each surface card is built from one of the ten labelled card shapes of a public diagnostic plate [3], taken as a pump card by assumption, at an assumed stroke and load, so the cards illustrate shapes rather than field wells. They are shown in Figure 2.

  • A round trip through the same rod model recovers every pump card within 0.4 to 2.4 % (root mean square) of its load range: a self-consistency check, not a validation.
  • The shape model names 6 of the 10 conditions correctly (5 firm, 1 by name mapping).
  • Where it misses, it reads tagging up, tagging down, plunger out of barrel and sand production as a traveling valve leak.

How the ten examples are built, and the full values, are given in Appendix C.

Run the analysis on measured cards

The same analysis runs in a web page in the digitalmodel library (version of 14 Nov 2026); it needs no installation and no network once its files are on disk. Load a surface card with the well's rod and pump data, and the page draws the pump card and reads fillage, fluid load and the model readings. Its guide gives the file format.

Appendix A Example well: inputs, results and limits

The example well behind Figure 1 and the key conclusions is summarised in this appendix.

  • The well is one anonymised field well in the digitalmodel library: a 1.500 in plunger at 4,844 ft on a two-taper steel rod string, pumped at 6.42 strokes a minute.
  • The inputs that matter most: a 1 % larger rod diameter lowers the maximum pump load by about 3.2 %, and a 1 % load-cell gain error raises it by about 2.6 %.
  • Unanchored tubing is not modelled and only steel rods are accepted. Both methods and the web page treat the well as vertical; a deviation survey is flagged, not modelled.

Inputs, results, sensitivity and limits in full: digitalmodel on GitHub, example-well.md.

Appendix B The two numerical methods compared

The key conclusion of the comparison of the two methods is given in this appendix.

  • A finite-difference method [2], the default, and a frequency-domain method [1] compute the pump card. On the example well their pump cards differ by 0.26 % (root mean square) of the load range, and each differs from the card stored in the well record by at most 0.78 %.
  • For this problem, at this accuracy, the methods are equivalent. All results in this article use the default method.
  • As more types of card come in, the two methods are kept side by side: some physical behaviour may be captured better by one method than by the other.

Methods and comparison in full: digitalmodel on GitHub, numerical-methods.md.

Appendix C The ten examples in detail

How the ten examples of Figure 2 are built is summarised in this appendix.

  • Each surface card starts from one of the ten shapes of the public diagnostic plate [3], taken as a pump card by assumption and scaled to an assumed stroke and load. It is lifted to the surface through the rod string of the example well and then analysed like a measured card.
  • The web page gives the same results as the library on all ten examples: the same model labels, and pump loads that differ by at most 3 × 10⁻⁹ lbf, a rounding-level difference.

Basis, assumptions and the full values of each example: digitalmodel on GitHub, ten-examples.md.
